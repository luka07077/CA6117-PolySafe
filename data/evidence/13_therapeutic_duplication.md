# Therapeutic duplication

Summary written for the PolySafe prototype. Sources: STOPP/START version 3 (Eur Geriatr Med 2023), criterion on duplicate drug class prescription; AGS Beers Criteria 2023.

## What therapeutic duplication means
Therapeutic duplication is the concurrent use of two or more drugs from the same pharmacological class, or with the same therapeutic purpose, when one would be sufficient. STOPP lists "any duplicate drug class prescription" (for example two concurrent NSAIDs, SSRIs, loop diuretics, ACE inhibitors or anticoagulants) as potentially inappropriate: optimisation of a single agent should be considered before adding a second drug of the same class.

## Why it happens
Duplication often results from care being split across several prescribers, from switching between drugs without stopping the first, from over-the-counter purchases under brand names (for example Advil and Aleve are both NSAIDs), and from hospital discharge medication lists that are not reconciled with existing prescriptions.

## Higher-risk duplications
- Two anticoagulants (for example warfarin plus a DOAC): very high bleeding risk; treated as critical.
- Two NSAIDs: additive GI and kidney toxicity.
- Two SSRIs: serotonin syndrome and bleeding.
- Two benzodiazepines: excessive sedation, falls and respiratory depression.
- Two sulfonylureas: hypoglycaemia.

## Lower-risk duplications
- Two acid-suppressing drugs (a PPI plus an H2-receptor antagonist): rarely needed and adds cost and adverse effects.
- Two dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers or two nitrates: usually an error; confirm intent.

## Not a duplication
Combinations from different classes used deliberately for additive effect (for example an ACE inhibitor plus a thiazide, or aspirin plus clopidogrel after a stent) are not duplications, although they may still interact.
