# Diabetes medicines: hypoglycaemia and volume depletion

Summary written for the PolySafe prototype. Sources: FDA prescribing information for fluconazole, clarithromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, insulin glargine (Lantus) and empagliflozin (Jardiance), Warnings and Drug Interactions sections; FDA Drug Safety Communication (July 2018): fluoroquinolones and blood-sugar disturbances; AGS Beers Criteria 2023.

## Sulfonylureas + fluconazole
Glipizide and gliclazide are metabolised by CYP2C9, which fluconazole inhibits. Sulfonylurea levels can rise and cause hypoglycaemia, which may be prolonged in older adults. Blood glucose should be monitored closely during the antifungal course.

## Sulfonylureas + clarithromycin
Clarithromycin has been associated with hypoglycaemia in patients taking sulfonylureas or insulin. Blood glucose should be monitored.

## Sulfonylureas + trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole
Sulfamethoxazole inhibits CYP2C9 and has a glucose-lowering effect of its own. The combination can cause hypoglycaemia, particularly in older adults and those with reduced kidney function.

## Sulfonylureas or insulin + ciprofloxacin
In 2018 FDA strengthened warnings that fluoroquinolones can cause serious blood-sugar disturbances, including hypoglycaemic coma, especially in older adults and patients taking oral hypoglycaemic drugs or insulin. Blood glucose should be monitored.

## Insulin + beta-blockers
Beta-blockers can mask the adrenergic warning symptoms of hypoglycaemia (tremor, palpitations), so hypoglycaemia may be recognised later. This is usually a minor interaction managed by patient counselling about other symptoms such as sweating and confusion.

## Empagliflozin (SGLT2 inhibitor) + loop diuretics
SGLT2 inhibitors cause osmotic diuresis. With furosemide the additive fluid loss can cause volume depletion, hypotension and acute kidney injury, especially in older adults. Volume status and kidney function should be monitored.

## Sulfonylureas in older adults
The 2023 Beers Criteria recommend avoiding sulfonylureas as first- or second-line diabetes therapy because of the risk of prolonged hypoglycaemia; if one is used, a short-acting agent is preferred. Two sulfonylureas at the same time are a therapeutic duplication.
